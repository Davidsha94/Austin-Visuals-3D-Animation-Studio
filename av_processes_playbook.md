### Sales

#### How AV handles new inbound leads
The sales docs are consistent on the basic shape of intake: ask budget first, collect the essentials, send a quick acknowledgment, then pull the team in to decide whether AV should quote the work. The main reason this matters is simple: AV does not want to spend hours pricing work that is either too small, too vague, or too risky to deliver. The sales notes also show that Matt wants enough information up front to avoid speculative work and to understand who is paying, what the audience is, and how polished the final piece needs to be. fileciteturn4file7 fileciteturn3file4

The part that still needs cleanup is the budget threshold. Some notes say AV will take projects at $1,000 and up. Another says under $2,000 should be referred out. The relationship-calling materials say not to spend time building relationships around old projects below $2,500. Those are not the same rule, so Tony should probably surface this as a policy conflict instead of pretending it is settled. fileciteturn3file2 fileciteturn3file4 fileciteturn5file0

#### How AV follows up on older leads
The relationship-calling program is less about closing on the first call and more about staying useful long enough to become the obvious choice later. The callers are supposed to reconnect with older leads, ask what happened, find out who won the work, and learn what the buyer values. If the person is already a client, the call also doubles as a way to collect ROI language, testimonials, and ideas for case studies. fileciteturn5file0 fileciteturn5file1

What can go wrong here is easy to see in the notes. There is no single follow-up cadence beyond “45 days,” “monthly,” or “call them back when it makes sense.” That means different callers could be handling the same type of lead differently. The spreadsheet fields are detailed, which helps, but the process still wants a single authoritative rhythm. fileciteturn5file0

#### How AV turns leads into proposals
Once a lead clears intake, the sales notes say AV gathers internal estimates, asks follow-up questions, checks whether the team can really hit the quality bar, and then builds the proposal on official letterhead. Across the proposals, you can see the same pattern repeated for medical, technical, character, and event work: project overview, objectives, deliverables, timeline, milestones, and budget. That is the core AV proposal engine. fileciteturn4file12 fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn2file3 fileciteturn4file15

One sales note explains this directly: if the client does not already have a strong concept, storyboard, or presentation structure, AV first works out the creative blueprint, then prices production more accurately. That is important because otherwise AV is guessing at house-construction costs before the house is designed. fileciteturn4file16

### Production

#### How projects actually start
The real start of production is not “client said yes.” It is “commercial terms are agreed, missing assets are gathered, and the team is mobilized.” The sales process docs make that explicit. AV agrees the start day and payment schedule, gets the deposit, asks for any remaining references or assets, and then Matt sends a high-priority email to the internal team, who are expected to acknowledge it and confirm they understand the assignment. fileciteturn4file12

This matters because the docs show AV relies heavily on fast internal communication. One note flatly says the company needs 95 percent communication reliability to succeed. The process also assumes someone is actively assigning roles, confirming tasks, and catching roadblocks before they become schedule failures. fileciteturn4file18

#### How client reviews are supposed to work
The cleaner proposals all push the same principle: review at milestone gates, get approvals early, and avoid letting late-stage revisions blow up the schedule. Delta says the team should not proceed beyond a milestone without sign-off. C-Vision says feedback should be consolidated into one response per round. Baylor says partial deliveries are approved step by step. This is one of the clearest through-lines in the whole document set. fileciteturn2file3 fileciteturn2file8 fileciteturn4file19

What can go wrong is also spelled out. Late changes to logos, wardrobe, props, scene composition, or newly requested shots can require a change order. For Tony, that means the public-facing AV Processes page should probably explain not just “we review in stages,” but “late decisions are expensive because they force rework.” fileciteturn4file19

#### How completed project files are handled
The outsourcing/ops instructions are clear on one critical point: a project is not really complete until AV has the artist’s working files. The operations assistant is supposed to contact the artist quickly, keep following up every three business days if needed, verify that the `Renders` folder contains the expected finals, and get Matt to confirm completeness before archiving. Then the job gets logged into the best-of spreadsheet and public links get added later if publicity is allowed. fileciteturn5file2

This process matters because it protects AV from losing source files, losing future revision ability, or paying out before the studio has control of the work product. It also feeds the downstream marketing machine: case studies, website uploads, and client-page entries all depend on this handoff working cleanly. fileciteturn5file2

#### How custom character work is de-risked
The Day & Night Plumbing proposal is especially useful because it explains how AV reduces risk on mascot animation. Before the 3D team starts, the client-side visuals need to be locked: front, side, and 3/4 views, expression sheets, object references, and proportion guides. The proposal also limits expression count and motion complexity to control price. fileciteturn4file9

This matters because character jobs are unusually vulnerable to scope creep. If expressions, props, or performance demands keep growing after the model build starts, the price and schedule stop being real.

#### How AV handles rush jobs
The C-Vision revision shows the rush-production philosophy in plain English. AV can compress a long normal schedule into four weeks, but only by charging rush fees, leaning on overtime, and demanding fast client decisions. The proposal even names the likely bottlenecks: horse names, livery colors, and race order. fileciteturn4file15 fileciteturn4file19

The big operational lesson is that rush success is never just an internal production problem. It depends just as much on the client locking decisions early and responding in consolidated review rounds.

### Marketing & Social Media

#### How social content is planned and published
The social media role doc and the outsource ops doc mostly agree on the goal: keep the channels active even when management is busy. The social manager is supposed to brainstorm in a shared Google Doc, get approval when possible, load content into the scheduler, and fall back to evergreen posts if new content is not ready in time. fileciteturn3file3 fileciteturn5file2

The conflict is tooling. One doc says the current scheduler is Meet Edgar. Another says Buffer is the active queue system and gives a cadence for queue reports. That needs to be reconciled before anyone treats this as a clean SOP. fileciteturn3file3 fileciteturn5file2

#### Why evergreen content matters so much
The ops notes are blunt: if nobody has created fresh content, the social queue still needs to stay full. That is why evergreen content is treated almost like operational inventory. The team is supposed to reuse posts that still make sense outside their original moment and avoid old posts that would now look outdated or weird. fileciteturn5file2

This matters because AV appears to rely on steady visibility more than on occasional viral spikes. The social media talk supports that too: the realistic business goal is sustained impressions and brand awareness, not hoping every clip explodes. fileciteturn1file9

#### How local SEO posts are structured
The Houston Google Business file makes the structure very visible: keyword, short title, short body, hashtags, then publish. It is repetitive by design. These posts are there to help AV show up for city + service combinations and cover a wide range of searches. fileciteturn4file1 fileciteturn4file13

The risk is that this can turn into content spam if there is no uniqueness standard. The docs support the tactic, but they do not define a quality threshold.

#### How blogs fit into the machine
The blog workflow is more strategic than the quick social posting system. It is designed to attract sophisticated buyers, build authority, and support SEO. The writer is supposed to pick a topic based on location, service, and industry, draft in Google Docs while learning, get David’s feedback, and only publish directly once quality is reliable. fileciteturn5file2

What matters most here is that AV does not want shallow content. The notes say the audience is smart and needs to learn something, not just be pitched at. That is a useful principle for the human-facing playbook.

### Legal

#### How confidentiality is handled
The NDA is straightforward: AV and the counterparty can exchange confidential information for project discussions, but neither side gets a free right to use that information outside the limited purpose. It runs for two years and uses Texas arbitration. fileciteturn2file2

The main gap is operational, not legal. The docs do not clearly say where signed NDAs live or how they are indexed.

#### How payment terms work in practice
Across the docs, payment terms are consistent in spirit but not in percentages. The sales-process documents say work does not begin until payment is received and mention one-third to one-half upfront. Baylor is a 50 percent deposit. C-Vision uses 50/25/25. The contractor agreement says AV often mirrors the client’s payment structure and that one-third / one-third / one-third is common. fileciteturn4file7 fileciteturn2file8 fileciteturn4file19 fileciteturn3file0

So the real rule seems to be: AV insists on up-front money before production, but the exact split varies by project. That should be documented as a policy with allowed patterns, not left implicit.

#### How contractors are governed
The Kevin Anderson contractor agreement is one of the strongest process docs in the set. It makes expectations explicit: no personal branding in client communication, daily organization and file uploads, twice-weekly project updates, watermark all client-facing previews, do not discuss budget with the client, and do not poach AV’s clients or vendors. fileciteturn3file0

This matters because the studio seems to rely on contractors heavily. Without this kind of rule set, AV would be vulnerable to communication drift, file chaos, and relationship damage.

### Admin & QA

#### How AV collects reviews and case studies
The review and case-study process is downstream from delivery and archiving. Once the project is complete and Matt approves outreach, someone packages the project info, gets David or Dan involved, and requests a review. Case studies come slightly later and get tracked in the best-of spreadsheet so the team knows what is public and what has already been turned into marketing material. fileciteturn5file2

This matters because a finished project is not just revenue. It is also future proof of competence if the client relationship and permissions are handled correctly.

#### How website and form QA is handled
The QA process is simple but important: test contact forms weekly, verify that the right inboxes got the messages, log failures, and escalate broken pages or odd form behavior to Matt with screenshots. Separately, browse around the sites for broken videos, bad links, or visible page issues. fileciteturn4file8

This matters because the marketing system only works if leads can actually reach the team. It is one of the most boring processes in the set and one of the highest leverage.

#### How AV keeps a client and school relationship pipeline
Two low-glamour admin processes stand out: adding completed clients to the public client page and maintaining college relationships for interns. Both depend on spreadsheets and periodic follow-up rather than fancy tools. That is not elegant, but it is consistent with the rest of the AV operating style in these docs. fileciteturn5file2 fileciteturn4file10

### Strategy

#### Where the company thought it needed to go
The old business-plan notes say something important that still fits the rest of the docs: AV wanted to stop relying on random one-off leads and move toward specialized markets or recurring agency/marketing-director relationships. The logic was that specialization improves pricing power, repeat work, and system clarity. fileciteturn4file11

That strategic thread shows up everywhere else too. The social posts are verticalized. The proposals are verticalized. The relationship-calling system favors bigger-budget, repeatable buyers. So even though the docs are operational, the strategy underneath them is pretty consistent.
